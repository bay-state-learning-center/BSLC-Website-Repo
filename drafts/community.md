The title of this post succinctly sums up what is hardest to convey about what we do at BSLC. And by "we" I mean everyone: staff, students, parents, volunteers, board members, and alumni. Modern discourse has cheapened the word "community."" When I use the word, I mean something of vital, almost sacred, importance, but regrettably it is the same word commonly used to characterize common interest groups for marketing purposes, as in "The Beanie Baby collector community" or "The international community of Star Trek fans." Those may well be fine things, but they are not characterized primarily by close personal relationships of mutual support, though such relationships may exist in those groups, the groups themselves are not communities.

By "community," I mean the sum of a growing web of close personal relationships of mutual support and trust between all participants past and future, full stop.


![Students at Bay State Learning Center drinking tea together in a classroom](teatime.webp)

That is something more than an institution. If we were merely an institution we would be defined by procedures and methods codified in specific documents, and it would be staff’s job to methodically carry out those procedures. A community is something different.

That is not to say there is anything wrong with institutions. The last few years of American political life have been a master class in the importance of institutions and what happens when institutions break down. Institutional rules and laws are the container that keeps communities safe and gives them a place to thrive. BSLC for example is a non-profit corporation with a board, and we follow the regulations laid down by Massachusetts state law. But that institutional structure is only the flower pot that the living, blooming community thrives in. The institutional container is necessary for the community to exist, but it is not the Community.  

This is one of the problems that plagues public education; too often it’s all institution and no community. All pot, no flower.

![Students at Bay State Learning Center hanging out in the common area](group_on_couch.webp)

The community at BSLC is a living organism, a system of relationships nurtured by the soil of a common culture that has been developed by every student and staff that has ever been at BSLC for over 13 years now. The distinctive culture of BSLC is composed of every member’s daily activities, (conversations, conflicts, resolutions, practices, sacrifices, kindnesses, commitments, and many other things) from BSLC’s first day to the present.

A rich culture takes time to build, just like a rich soil takes time to develop. It is exactly this combination of culture and community that is essential to education; curriculums, regulations, and administrative rules can be useful, but they are not essential. Education is not primarily about resources or texts either; you can read lots of books, but until you discuss them with friends you love and trust, you haven’t begun to learn what books have to teach. The same holds true of any kind of learning from music to mathematics.

![Students at Bay State Learning Center prepare for a mock trial](mock_trial.webp)

All the greatest leaps of discovery in human learning were explored and discovered by friends in a close knit learning community. There are lots of examples from the Agora of ancient Athens, to Nalanda University in Medieval India to the religious and healing tradition of the Ojibwe Mediwiwin, they all thrive on close relationships. To take a modern example, I recommend reading a few of the letters between Neils Bohr and Albert Einstein, they participated in a lively community of theorists and mathematicians, and their epistolary disputes over quantum physics were legendary. However, they were also lifelong friends and their rivalry was famously playful and intellectually honest. Einstein remarked that when he first met Bohr in 1920 that he had never met someone who brought him such joy simply by existing. Their letters are brimming with admiration and playful teasing. When asked about their relationship Einstein said,

"Nobody knows how the stand of our knowledge about the atom would be without him. Personally, Bohr is one of the most amiable colleagues I have met. He utters his opinions like one perpetually groping and never like one who believes himself to be in possession of the truth."

![Neils Bohr and Albert Einstein in conversation](Bohr-vs-Einstein.webp)

The 20th century was marked by amazing scientific discoveries because of a system of close personal relationships like Einstein and Bohr’s, (and Max Born, Werner Heisenberg, Erwin Schrodinger, et al.). Funding and institutional structure alone would never have yielded such fruitful discussions--and sometimes those things were an impediment to fruitful discussion. 

Our conversations at BSLC may not be as grandly historic, but they are every bit as fruitful for our small community, and that is why every class is a conversation. It is also the reason students come to class even when there is no institutional requirement that they do so. Community drives education, indeed, it is the only thing that ever has.
